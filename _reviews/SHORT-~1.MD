## 29 December 2025 — My 2025 reading round-up

I'm pleased to report that, after a long hiatus due to study and travel, my self-imposed program of relentless reading is back on track. It is sobering to think that if you're in your mid-thirties and read only one book a month, you'll manage perhaps 500 more books in your lifetime. That's not many books. A grim and terrifying prospect. Happy holidays, everyone!

### *Life and Fate* (1960) by Vasily Grossman

Like Bulgakov's *The Master and Margarita*, this is a novel that just barely managed to escape the clutches of the Soviet regime and which its author never saw published in his lifetime. Crudely put, it is the Battle of Stalingrad told as a sprawling Tolstoyan family epic. It is unwieldy. It is unbalanced. At times cruel and shocking. At other times humane and beautiful. I'm glad (relieved, in fact!) that I finally got around to reading it.

### *The Collected Short Stories of Roberto Bolaño* (2007) by Roberto Bolaño

Exactly what it says on the tin. "Last Evenings on Earth" is an incredibly badass short story. The saddest thing about this collection is that it includes his many incomplete posthumous stories, which read as though he was just about to mature into a new stage of his writing. He died too soon.

### *Letters to a Young Poet* (1929) by Rainer Maria Rilke

Eh. Possibly an issue with the English translation, but this didn't resonate with me.

### *The Road: Stories, Journalism, and Essays* (1987) by Vasily Grossman

The terror of Stalin's purges seen through the eyes of a young girl. The horrors of the Eastern Front seen through the eyes of a mule. Grossman's journalistic account — almost certainly the first on the Allied side — of the Nazi extermination camps is told with feverish precision. Excellent throughout. More people should be aware of Grossman.

### *Solenoid* (2015) by Mircea Cărtărescu

Imagine combining the unrestrained, childlike fantasies of Bruno Schulz with the cosmic horror of H.P. Lovecraft, then throwing in the ramblings of a possibly schizophrenic Romanian schoolteacher. Now imagine doing this for nearly 900 pages. This is one deeply strange novel. The relentless insect metaphors grew tiresome for me, and I wished it dug deeper into its satirical treatment of life under Communist Romania. An avant-garde literary challenge that retains (some) coherency.

### *Rejection* (2024) by Tony Tulathimutte

A collection of short stories obsessively ruminating on rejection in the modern world. The first story, "The Feminist" — a ferocious satire imagining a hard left-wing feminist transforming into a violent incel — is impressive. I found the second entry ("Pics") quite upsetting, and the third ("Ahegao") filthy and hilarious. Unfortunately, the rest of the collection runs out of steam.

### *The Slave* (1962) by Isaac Bashevis Singer

Pretty good overall. Think Jewish magical realism. Imagine seventeenth-century Polish peasant life as an exotic, inscrutable realm. If that combination sounds intriguing, then you'll have a good time.

### *Selfish Reasons to Have More Kids* (2011) by Bryan Caplan

Mostly solid. More people would benefit from reading things that make them slightly uncomfortable. Mood affiliation is mind poison.

### *The Screwtape Letters* (1942) by C.S. Lewis

I enjoyed this immensely, despite my unrepentant prejudice that Anglicanism produces the dullest strain of Christian thought (i.e., the British kind). Lewis's theological imagination is jolly good fun.

### *Africa: The Definitive Visual History of a Continent* (2024)

Gave me the pleasure of learning a few more facts about Africa.

### *Valuable Humans in Transit and Other Stories* (2022) by qntm

Serviceable science fiction written for the Internet generation. The best stories in the collection are available online for free.

### *The Sailor Who Fell from Grace with the Sea* (1963) by Yukio Mishima

A washed-up merchant seaman and a gang of sociopathic schoolboys grasp for meaning in this shocking novella. Real men read Mishima to indulge their Freudian death drive. I went back afterwards and skimmed Mishima's *The Temple of the Golden Pavilion* again, which I now appreciate much more on a second pass.

### *The Loneliness of Sonia and Sunny* (2025) by Kiran Desai

The emotionally torturous courtship of two Indians living abroad in America. Above average. I've read critics who defend the book's middling prose in favour of its overall tonal effect, but I couldn't excuse it so easily. You cannot write entire paragraphs composed solely of rhetorical questions and expect to get away with it more than a couple of times. For some reason, it made me want to revisit Min Jin Lee's much more masterful *Pachinko*.

I'm also mostly done with *Snow Country* by Yasunari Kawabata, which I've found restrained to the point of being mostly boring. I was hoping for something closer to his bizarre lesbian revenge novella *Beauty and Sadness*, but alas, that was not the case.

## 13 December 2022 — My top eight book picks for 2022 (unranked)

### *The Savage Detectives* (1998) by Roberto Bolaño

A semi-autobiographical epic by the late, offbeat Chilean scribbler Roberto Bolaño and a love note to Latin American literature. It's (initially) about two obscure poets floating through the sometimes ludicrous, often sybaritic, occasionally violent world of bohemian writers in 1970s Mexico City. And then it spirals from there. Honestly, it's one of the best novels I've read.

As I descended into it, an alien feeling came over me. I thought to myself: 'Hmm. Maybe life is worth living. Maybe I should stick it out and see what happens next.' There's so much visceral, all-embracing life described in this book(!) And what does it all mean? My interpretation is it's about two supremely important ideas: (1) It's about 'polútropos': the painful wanderings and many-turnings of humanity, and (2) it's about the end of the world, the slow-approaching apocalypse. That's right.

### *My Struggle: Book 1* (2009) by Karl Ove Knausgaard

I lie. I didn't read it. I listened to it as a 16-hour audiobook while sitting in the dark, half-blind, mildly opiated, and recovering from laser eye surgery. It was dreamy.

This Norwegian series has a frightful literary reputation. I mean, from the title choice alone, you know you are in for some Bold Shit. Each entry is a brutally confessional retelling of major chapters from the author's life, packaged with novelistic bowstrings. Infamously, after they were published, his (ex-)wife had a mental breakdown and his family was hounded by the media. As I said, Bold Shit.

Book 1 was extremely good. In it, Knausgaard recounts his journey to young adulthood and his bitter relationship with his father. The second half of Book 1 is singularly focused on a deeply tragic episode in Knausgaard's life and executes it with such punishing detail that I started to feel distressed at points.

### *The Death of Ivan Ilyich and Other Stories* by Leo Tolstoy

Not a lot here for me to say. These are very cherished stories in international literature. Many readers expound on how profoundly moving they found "The Death of Ivan Ilyich" or they rave about "Hadji Murad".

Personally, I enjoyed Tolstoy's depiction of the Imperial Russian campaign against the Chechens in "A Prisoner in the Caucasus", the shocking values dissonance (for modern minds) in "The Kreutzer Sonata" and the uniquely Tolstoyan vision of Christian living in "Father Sergius". And, of course, no one can refute the triumph that is "Master and Man", a formidable tale about what it means to be human in the icy face of death.

### *My Struggle: Book 2* (2009) by Karl Ove Knausgaard

Not as hard-hitting as Book 1 but nevertheless compelling. Book 2 dives into Knausgaard's experience with fatherhood, his new life in Stockholm, his courtship of his second wife, and — this was my favourite element — his unusual friendship with Geir, a wilful, somewhat chauvinistic fellow writer. Also, he hilariously moans about how much he dislikes Swedish culture. It's a fantastic, messy journey and I look forward to eventually mustering the stamina to tackle Books 3 through 6.

The author claims the *My Struggle* series is about the banality of life. I disagree! My view is that the books are about how we confront and navigate authentic moments of beauty and profundity in our prosaic lives, especially when it is far easier to hide under the comfortable banality of our daily grind.

### *The Power and the Glory* (1940) by Graham Greene

Did you know that in the 1930s the Mexican government tried to crush and eliminate the Catholic Church? And that the state of Tabasco had an especially murderous anti-clerical regime?

Within this charged setting, Greene offers a compelling story of a fallen, alcoholic priest on a desperate quest to escape persecution. And at the same time, this disgraced priest must contend with his wavering faith and his unavoidable duties. Near the end, the novel rushes towards an inevitable finale where obligation and dignity clash against a person's raw will to survive. It's heady stuff.

### *The Last Samurai* (2000) by Helen DeWitt

No, it has nothing to do with that damn Tom Cruise movie! DeWitt's debut novel is an inventive and idiosyncratic tale about a highly intelligent, mentally unsound woman who attempts to raise (or rather, create) a child prodigy while living alone in poverty.

I did not like some sections, but I cannot deny that it's a wonderfully special work of fiction and, as such, will recommend it to everybody. It's about language, travel, education, film, music, pedagogy, and a kaleidoscope of other topics. It's a book by a polymath that seeks to inspire others to be a tiny bit more polymathematical. Give it a try.

### *Moby-Dick* (1851) by Herman Melville

Possibly the oddest of the Big Classic Novels. It's bombastic, it's Shakespearean, it's an account of 19th-century whaling methods, it's an allegory for the American political experiment, it's about Biblical lessons, it's openly homoerotic, it has childish jokes, and fundamentally it's about a wild crew of sailors on a doomed globetrotting quest and at one point one of them cuts off a whale's foreskin and wears it as a raincoat.

What makes *Moby-Dick* so great can be expressed, funnily enough, with a Knausgaard quote. In Book 2 of *My Struggle*, he has this passage:

> When I read Lucretius it's all about the magnificence of the world... It's about things. The physicality of things. Animals. Trees. Fish... I write to recapture the world. Yes, not the world I'm in. Definitely not the social world. The wonder-rooms of the Baroque age. The curiosity cabinets. And the world in Kiefer's trees. That's art. Nothing else.

*Moby-Dick* is great because all its intellectual drama and philosophical musings are inseparably welded to a grand, unknowable, and — most crucially — physical, natural universe. One that is no longer.

### *The Remains of the Day* (1993) by Kazuo Ishiguro

It's a quaint premise. A butler from a dignified estate drives a car through the English countryside. He reminisces about his life from a bygone era. He says gentlemanly sentiments and sits on a bench overlooking the ocean.

And why is it so satisfying? Ishiguro uses this framing to explore the inescapable questions we all ask ourselves: How should we live in the shadow of vast personal regrets? How can we carry on in the face of 'what-could-have-beens'? Everyone ponders these questions on their darker days and everyone hopes to emerge from these musings a wiser individual.

## 9 December 2022 — Honourable and dishonourable mentions

Before I post my personal top book picks of 2022, I'd like to share some honourable/dishonourable mentions:

- ***All the Pretty Horses*** by Cormac McCarthy. Cowboys! Romance! Adventure! I haven't actually finished it yet, but it'll probably slide in as an unofficial #9 top book. McCarthy's prose, when he is at the peak of his powers, is glorious to read and reread.
- ***Lightning Rods*** by Helen DeWitt. A satirical novella about capitalism, sex, and (weirdly enough) the Protestant work ethic. Late-nineties wit and chutzpah that was sadly published in 2011, well past its prime.
- ***Graveyard Clay*** by Máirtín Ó Cadhain. About petty Irish ghosts bickering in the afterlife. Considered one of the greatest books ever written in Gaelic. I found the English translation pleasant, though only mildly engaging.
- ***The Temple of the Golden Pavilion*** by Yukio Mishima. About obsession, sacred objects, and destructive masculinity as artistic expression. I'm scared to criticise it since I know a bunch of Japanese literature fans will jump out and knife me. It was, um, above average.
- ***Atomised*** by Michel Houellebecq. A rageful tale about how secular, sexually permissive Europe will eat itself alive. O, Houellebecq, you dirty woman-loathing asshole. I loved *Submission*, but this was just too extreme in its misanthropy. Occasionally moving, though.
- ***Companion Piece*** by Ali Smith. About overcoming the darkest days of the COVID-19 pandemic through imagination. Or something. Meh.
- ***The Kite Runner*** by Khaled Hosseini. Modern Afghan history; suffering and survival; Bildungsroman. A beloved novel, and rightly so, but I kept seeing high school English essay questions flashing before my eyes as I read it.
- ***A Thousand Splendid Suns*** by Khaled Hosseini. See above, but this time with female characters. Unexpectedly nuanced in its portrayal of certain aspects of the Taliban regime.
- ***Return of a King*** by William Dalrymple. About the First Anglo–Afghan War and how the British were very, very dumb. Why the hell did I read three books set in Afghanistan this year? You can't go wrong with Dalrymple, though I prefer his Indian history books more.
- ***The Piazza Tales*** by Herman Melville. His most famous short stories are in this collection ("Benito Cereno", "The Encantadas"). They generally hold up despite the dated prose. Look, we all have to read "Bartleby the Scrivener" at some point, right?
- ***The Trouble with Being Born*** by Emil Cioran. A collection of comedic, nihilistic aphorisms by this mighty and miserable Romanian philosopher. I found a few of them dastardly astute, although I'm dubious of Cioran's merit as a focused, consistent thinker.
- ***The Life of Samuel Johnson*** by James Boswell. Likely the most influential biography ever written. I read an abridged version since life is short. If you have a cursory interest in this renowned 18th-century man of letters, then I think it's worth your while.

I won't be posting a separate top movie list this year since I was underwhelmed by most of the films I watched. None of these are hidden gems or should come as a surprise.

*Butch Cassidy and the Sundance Kid* was a fantastic watch. *The Lost Daughter* cements the extraordinary filmability of Elena Ferrante's writings. *Y tu mamá también* was a goddamn delight and I'm sad I didn't watch it sooner. And *Mishima: A Life in Four Chapters* was my favourite film of the year – a masterpiece of cinema.

## 17 December 2021 — Top eight books I read in 2021

### 1. *Submission* (2015) by Michel Houellebecq

The setting is deliberately inflammatory: the year is 2022 and an Islamist party is about to take power in France. It is startling, problematic, uncomfortable, and a very, very entertaining novel. Don't confuse it for mere Islamophobic satire though. Houellebecq's true target is the spineless intelligentsia that he sees as complicit bystanders of a Europe slipping into the spiritual void.

### 2. *Shame* (1983) by Salman Rushdie

*Shame* tells the grotesque power struggle between two elite families. In a nutshell, it is an allegory about the dysfunctional birth and maturation of the modern Pakistani nation-state, told through a magical realist lens. It is certainly his most thematically tight and structurally focussed novel. It also felt like his most reflective and meditative work. My favourite Rushdie novel.

### 3. *Stories of Your Life and Others* (2010) by Ted Chiang

A brilliant collection of science fiction short stories. In an interview, Ezra Klein rightly described him as a great writer of religious fiction (even though Chiang is an atheist), which makes his works even more fascinating. My favourite stories in the collection: "Tower of Babylon", "Story of Your Life" (which was adapted into Villeneuve's film *Arrival*) and "Hell is the Absence of God".

### 4. *Exhalation* (2019) by Ted Chiang

Another great collection from a very unique and talented thinker. As with *Stories of Your Life and Others*, these stories are fantastical, philosophical, and deeply humanistic despite often involving decidedly non-human entities. My favourite stories here were: "The Merchant and the Alchemist's Gate", "The Great Silence", and "The Lifecycle of Software Objects".

### 5. *Immune* (2021) by Philipp Dettmer

From the head writer of a popular science education YouTube channel. A fantastic introduction to the incredibly elegant and absurdly complex mechanisms of the human immune system for general audiences. As the book itself notes, the sobering truth is that our immune system is the reason we're all alive and, when it ultimately fails, will be the cause of death for many of us.

### 6. *Maus* (1980) by Art Spiegelman

The classic black-and-white graphic novel. About the Holocaust. With cartoon mice. Not only does it manage to be one of the most harrowing true survival stories that I've read on this subject, but it is also a compelling personal account of the complex, difficult relationship between Spiegelman and his own father, the core subject of the novel.

### 7. *Under the Volcano* (1947) by Malcolm Lowry

Told over the events of a single day, at face value this is a novel about a man drinking himself into both literal and spiritual damnation in a small town in Mexico. Love, loss, and intense self-medication is the name of the game. It is highly symbolic, at times very difficult to parse, and dense with references. The ending, despite its apparent inevitability, is still shocking.

### 8. *As I Lay Dying* (1930) by William Faulkner

"My mother is a fish." The Bundrens, a hapless rural family in the Deep South decide to take the body of their family matriarch to be buried in her hometown. The journey is arduous, tragic, and absurd, and reveals the inner lives and secrets of a truly flawed and disturbed family. It's a short novel and the first piece of Faulkner I've read. I was quite impressed by it.

Note that I excluded *Ulysses* since it wasn't so much a book for me and more of an obligatory literary experience. Anyway, what's next? Going to work through Tolstoy's short stories early next year. Might try more Houellebecq. Might look into Domenico Starnone (who may or may not be, along with his partner, the actual Elena Ferrante). Always open to recommendations. Hit me up.

## 17 December 2021 — Top eight films I watched this year

Truth be told, this was a fairly flat year. I didn't find many of the movies I watched that interesting (admittedly, that's probably because I blitzed through my list of international films in last year's lockdown). Anyway, here's my end-of-year list in no particular order.

### 1. *Miller's Crossing* (1990), dir. Joel and Ethan Coen

A beautiful and intelligently crafted film, with just enough of the Coen brothers' signature quirks to make it feel enriched but not distracting. I'd happily call it one of my favourite gangster films, and certainly my favourite of their "pre-*Fargo*" filmography (I just didn't feel that much for *Barton Fink*, sorry).

### 2. *Pig* (2021), dir. Michael Sarnoski

An emotional story that never relies on shortcuts or treats the audience like morons. Nicolas Cage! What a performance! While the premise is a bit... odd (is Portland really the right setting for cut-throat restauranteering and long-lost legendary chefs?), the themes of grief, reconciliation, and acceptance rise above it.

### 3. *Still Life* (2006), dir. Jia Zhangke

Loved it. Probably my top movie on this list and it is certainly Zhangke's masterpiece. Set in a small town slowly being destroyed by the construction of the Three Gorges Dam, this film is a powerful witness to those left behind by China's relentless drive for progress. And, as in all his films, Zhao Tao is a thrill to see onscreen.

### 4. *Dune* (2021), dir. Denis Villeneuve

It's far from a perfect film but it's a hell of a cinematic experience when it rises to the occasion. As a long-time fan of the novel who has suffered the eccentric Lynch adaptation *and* the outlandish Sci Fi Channel miniseries, I was delighted to see Villeneuve handle the material with pure expertise. I eagerly await the sequel.

### 5. *The Long Goodbye* (1973), dir. Robert Altman

A damn slick film. Why wasn't Elliott Gould a leading man in more films? Transposing Chandler's hardboiled universe into the sleazy rump of 1970s Hollywood was an act of brilliance. It's subversive and caustically funny to all the right levels. Also, the second film on this list to riff on the famous final scene from *The Third Man*.

### 6. *Brokeback Mountain* (2005), dir. Ang Lee

A masterful drama. I read the film as being about individual yearnings vs. familial/societal obligations (a common theme in Asian cinema), with the subject of sexuality more of a secondary matter. After all, its first act evokes Hexagram 4 of the I Ching (mountain over water), which speaks of youthful folly and success.

### 7. *House of Flying Daggers* (2004), dir. Zhang Yimou

While I still maintain that Zhang Yimou sold out after the nineties (his pre-2000s filmography is marvellous), this is one of his stronger wuxia films. The costumes! The landscapes! The music! The colours! (Oh, the colours!) The film's depiction of a fantastical and tragic Jiang Hu world set in the Tang Dynasty is, at times, bewitching.

### 8. *Apocalypse Now* (1979 theatrical cut), dir. Francis Ford Coppola

Previously, my only foray into this film had been the bloated Redux cut and I couldn't stand it. I'm happy to say this is not the case with the theatrical release. Sometimes leaner is better. The descent into madness is more compelling. The hallucinatory war sequences are more focused. And that ending hits harder. This is the end, alright.

And some honourable mentions:

- *Sorcerer* by William Friedkin — sweaty, nihilistic, with an amazing bridge sequence.
- *Evangelion: 3.0+1.0 Thrice Upon a Time* by Hideaki Anno — nonsensical yet satisfying and thank god the fucking story is finally over.
- *Synecdoche, New York* by Charlie Kaufman — impressive, but too up-its-own-ass.
- *I'm Thinking of Ending Things* by Charlie Kaufman — much better than his debut and less up-its-own-ass.
- *Playtime* by Jacques Tati — conceptually sweet, just difficult to watch in a single sitting.
- *I, Tonya* by Craig Gillespie — a more engaging, thought-provoking story than I expected.
- *Hard Boiled* by John Woo — hell yeah!

## 13 December 2021 — Books that didn't make my end-of-year list

This was a very productive reading year. I clocked over 8,000 pages (according to Goodreads) and filed away some Chunky Dead White Male Classics. And none of them made my 2021 top list! Here are my remarks on books that didn't make the cut:

- ***Crime and Punishment*** by Fyodor Dostoyevsky – The novel where there's not enough crime or punishment, and too much monologuing in small rooms. But when Dostoyevsky hits his stride, he is positively fiery. An intriguing window into the cultural motions of 19th century urban Russia.
- ***Flights*** by Olga Tokarczuk – Maybe it was the English translation, but there was something ever so slightly uncaptivating in her travel-themed vignettes. Helped me respect the art of human anatomical preservation, though. *Primeval and Other Times* is a stronger work.
- ***From Rebel to Ruler*** by Tony Saich – The so-called definitive history of the Chinese Communist Party over the last one hundred years. Saich tackles its complex history adeptly, but I honestly cannot tell you whether I now completely understand the CCP and its legacy or understand it even less.
- ***Midnight's Children*** by Salman Rushdie – Despite its monumental acclaim, I was frustrated with its unrelenting length and repetitive, bombastic narration. Some vibrant, hilarious sequences with a genuine sense of place and history, but I didn't get much out of it by the end.
- ***My Name Is Red*** by Orhan Pamuk – I love the art of Islamic miniatures and learning about the Ottoman Empire, but boy did Pamuk push his luck in terms of novel-length. A competent, relatively unique story that could have been told with half the ink.
- ***Nobody Leaves: Impressions of Poland*** by Ryszard Kapuściński – Simply and utterly wonderful. A few short pieces I've read before and a few that were new to me. When are we getting an English translation of his *Lapidarium* series, goddammit!
- ***Nostromo*** by Joseph Conrad – Although it drags in large chunks and you get tired of reading about the silver-treasure-this and the silver-treasure-that, Conrad's convincing construction of an entirely fictional South American polity is worthy of admiration. Better than *Heart of Darkness*, I dare say.
- ***Notes from the Underground*** by Fyodor Dostoyevsky – A short, bitter, and brilliantly choleric study of an alienated modern soul. It was not too long and not too short. I found it a bit unengaging in the first half with its pure philosophical ranting, but the tangible "memoir" section was excellent.
- ***Open Borders*** by Bryan Caplan and Zach Weinersmith – A passionate, gallantly constructed libertarian argument for unrestricted immigration. As a trained economist, I have a soft spot for these kinds of Overton-Window-pushing proposals, though I recognise the case isn't perfect.
- ***The Heart of a Dog*** by Mikhail Bulgakov – Early Soviet-era satire. I respected it for its goofy pseudo-scientific premise and its jabs at the Bolshevik vision of a new humanity. In hindsight, it comes across as a tragic cultural artefact from just before the regime nosedived into pure terror and repression.
- ***The Life You Can Save*** by Peter Singer – Yeah, it's cool. Like it or not, Singer is Australia's most influential intellectual export, period. For full disclosure, I donate a small proportion of my monthly salary to The Life You Can Save's list of recommended charities, so make of that what you will.
- ***The Satanic Verses*** by Salman Rushdie – Impressively written in parts, though I found it structurally unbalanced. The best chapters, oddly enough, are the hilariously blasphemous Mahound dream sequences that landed Rushdie with a fatwa and a million death threats.

## 28 December 2020 — End-of-year top ten films

Cinema is dead! Long live cinema! Here's my obligatory end-of-year top ten films list. In no particular order:

1. *The Guilty* (2018): Danish high-concept thriller focussed entirely on one person sitting in a room. It is utterly gripping from the moment the plot kicks in till the moment the credits roll.
2. *Yi Yi* (2000): A wonderfully tender Taiwanese slice-of-life drama about a modern-day middle-class Taipei family during a very challenging time in their lives.
3. *Free Solo* (2018): Everyone told me to watch it and I didn't regret it. A superb character study. I was squirming on the couch during its most vertigo-inducing moments.
4. *Thunder Road* (2018): A low budget comedy-drama about a psychologically damaged police officer struggling in a small American town. Deeply moving by the end.
5. *Feels Good Man* (2020): A documentary on the Pepe the Frog meme! It manages to be insightful, hilarious, and at times surreal, even for those familiar with the troubled subject matter.
6. *Mulholland Drive* (2001): David Lynch at his Lynchiest. The emotional swells of the story and the chemistry between the two female leads overwhelms even its most cryptic sequences.
7. *Ran* (1985): Kurosawa's epic adaptation of *King Lear*. Incredible visuals coupled with chaotic, unforgiving energy. Perhaps my favourite Shakespeare adaptation to date.
8. *The Act of Killing* (2012): An unbelievably haunting documentary about the brutal Indonesian Communist Purge. At its essence, it is about whether art can unravel man's inhumanity to man.
9. *Uncut Gems* (2019): A grubby, anxiety-inducing thriller that feels like a train is ramming through your skull. Like everyone else, I enjoy hating on Adam Sandler, but he's impressively good in this.
10. I, uh, can't decide on a tenth film. I'll make it a three-way tie between *Tangerine* (2015), *Twelve Angry Men* (1957) and *The Lighthouse* (2019). Three completely incomparable films.

## 8 September 2020

Just finished *Summer*, the final book in Ali Smith's Seasonal Quartet. A wonderful, interconnected series where each book manages to simultaneously be a sturdy slice-of-life family drama, a bittersweet meditation on the importance of art, and an urgent reminder of the need to confront contemporary political challenges.

The first book caused a moderate stir in 2016 when it managed to become the first 'post-Brexit' novel, reflecting on that particular issue. Written with equal fervour, this fourth and final entry has similarly achieved, by my reckoning, the status of being the first piece of literary fiction partly set during the COVID-19 pandemic.

And with that, the beginning meets the end and the seasons come full circle with the remarkable literary accomplishment of this Quartet. Can happily recommend these books to anyone looking for something to read during the two-week Melbourne lockdown extension.

## 11 November 2019 — Ryszard Kapuściński

With 2019 nearing its inevitable tailspin, I am happy to report that I've accomplished my literary goal of reading every major English translated work by Polish writer and journalist Ryszard Kapuściński. Now all I have to do is brush up on my polski so I can complete his oeuvre.

### *The Soccer War* (1990)

Like *Travels with Herodotus*, this book is a grab bag of his adventures around the world — but with an emphasis on his experience as a journalist witnessing civil wars and violent regime change throughout the Global South. But unlike his previous works, in which he depicts himself as a meek and poetic soul, in *The Soccer War* he comes across as a reckless maniac. In one incident during the Nigerian Civil War, he describes driving solo into hostile roadblocks and getting robbed, assaulted, and nearly set on fire. In another, he writes about how he and his colleagues smuggled themselves into the Congo, were caught, and nearly executed by Belgian paratroopers. Harrowing *if* true — with italics around the *if*. I'll certainly say that Kapuściński was more a hearsayist than a rigorous fact-checker. In one unusual passage, he recounts the suicide of Mario Terán, the Bolivian army sergeant who executed Che Guevara. The only problem is that Terán is very much still alive to this day!

The best passage in *The Soccer War*, in which he underscores how absurdly volatile some parts of the world are:

> In the course of one month I had driven through five countries. In four of them, there were states of emergency... Two parliaments had been dissolved. Two governments had fallen. Scores of political activists had been arrested. Scores of people had been killed in political conflicts. ## 6 August 2018 — Ash Is Purest White

Watched *Ash Is Purest White* last night at the Melbourne International Film Festival and it was a bloated—though occasionally entertaining—muddle of a movie.

*Ash Is Purest White* is supposedly a sprawling crime drama about how power, loyalty, and sacrifice are tenuous concepts in contemporary China. 2. Time skip!
3. Oh no, all the characters are lost and sad because China has changed too quickly...

Whereas the first two-thirds has some slickly acted and choreographed scenes, as well as some sharp moments of humour, the final third is unjustifiably tedious. The screenplay ran out of ideas. Plus, it has possibly the most boring gangsters in any movie I've ever seen. All you see them do is play mahjong, chain smoke, eat noodles, and drink baijiu. Gangsters don't do that in China. Everyone does that in China.

This was disappointing since the director, Jia Zhangke, has made some really interesting films. Similarly, *Mountains May Depart* has a deeply ambitious story and includes a bizarre third act set in a futuristic Melbourne where the PTV trams are slightly bigger and firearm ownership is legal in Australia again (somehow).

All in all, like Chinese history itself, the film is too damn long.

## 4 February 2018 — Altered Carbon

So I just binged all ten episodes of *Altered Carbon* on Netflix. I have an addictive personality and my eyes sting a bit. Here's a review!

**Based** on a relatively obscure 2002 cyberpunk novel, this is an ultra-violent, high-concept, big-budget serialised TV drama that would have been impossible to make ten years ago. As such, I was very excited about it and it has *largely* met my expectations.

**The setting** is classic 'hardboiled cyberpunk' to a fault. Drunk detectives; dystopian mega-cities; flying cars; neon lights; rogue hackers; oppressive corporations; the future sucks and it's raining all the time. Yes, they're genre clichés, but they sure do remind you a bit of the world in 2018...

**The big idea** the show explores is this: Death has been cured. Everyone has what is essentially a 'hard-drive' near the base of their brain, so that if they die, they can just 'reinstall' their consciousness into another body. As it turns out, however, the consequences of technological immortality can be fucking horrifying.

**Rating-wise,** I'd give the first half of the season a solid 7/10 for staunchly committing to its premise. The acting was okay and the action was serviceable, though the dialogue was often a little too on-the-nose. If anything else, they went all in on the set design and costumes, turning it into the *Blade Runner* homage you secretly always wanted (you goddamn nerd).

**That said,** the second half of the season is a 5/10 as the third act devolves into a cheesy, melodramatic mess of weirdly paced character interactions and story ideas. Still watchable, but you might need to pour yourself a stiff drink.

**A warning** in case this sort of thing puts you off: The level of abject neo-noir violence committed against women in this show can get quite intense (on par with films like *Sin City*). It certainly began to leave a sour aftertaste for me towards the end. (Though the main screenwriter for the show is a woman, oddly enough.)

**Overall,** I'd say try one or two episodes if you're either (A) into bleak science fiction spectacle, (B) interested in unsettling, existential ideas about humanity and technology, or (C) waiting for season three of *The Expanse* to come out later this year.

As an addendum, here are some creepy, existential-crisis-inducing ideas that shows like *Altered Carbon* have explored. My crazy guess is that at least one or two of these could become actual issues within our lifetimes:

1. Technologies that can sufficiently alter human sensory perceptions as to make people question reality.
2. Total corporate ownership of biological components of the human body, or of technologies deeply integrated into the body.
3. The ability to torture or harm the neurological state of individuals without actually rendering permanent damage to their bodies.
4. With sufficiently advanced computing power, the inability to distinguish between an actual "conscious" (whatever that means) digital entity, and merely an advanced program that can mimic human conscious interactions.
5. A relationship between growing life expectancy/healthspan and aggressive wealth concentration. Similarly, a "genetic apartheid" emerging in society as a result of access to technologies such as gene-therapy.

Might as well start getting worried now, right?? Now, where did I leave my smartphone with all my personal data.....
