## 18 December 2019 — A balcony in Kuala Lumpur

Mary Oliver writes in her poem 'In Malaysia': "in the carved door / blossoms / yellow ivory pure white / swell and begin to open / the agony of their sweetness / far older than the house / far older than the city itself"

\~\~\~

It's quiet this morning on the balcony, overlooking colonial-era buildings and a courtyard strewn with rubble and moss. An old Chinese man is reading a newspaper behind me. Languid Kuala Lumpur. Already a brothy heat. I nurse my coffee. I've noticed that the locals become plumper in their happiness and the backpackers become gaunter in theirs.

Down below, a downspout is spewing brown water into an overflowing metal bin. Waste bags propagate in a darkling storeroom. Everywhere, corrugated patchwork rooftops. Blue, grey, green, red. Like shades of leaves, shades of petals.

Trash versus tropical growth. An endless battle. A sapling yearns from the gunk in the concrete guttering. Ferns burst from air conditioning units that cling like dirty barnacles to the walls. A cat lurks the weedy thickness behind the clotheslines. Roots, green and vermiform, slither from cracks above me. Concrete is an invention for dry summers and mild winters, not the raging, infinitely breeding tropics. Such a static thing could never win against so much aching life.

Time for a poem.

\~\~\~

under this tin corrugated  
ramshackle roof  
blue in a way they can only paint in the tropics  
the downpour is like a river of thunderclaps  
cymbals dancing atop one another

within this deafening house  
there must be a grandmother snoring next to the tv  
a pot simmering into evening  
a dog cursing his god given ears

and a child  
imagining what sound snow would  
make in his world —

like the paws of a cat?  
or the whole sky humming down  
with an even more mysterious music

## 12 December 2019 — Penang

Ah, yes. The awkwardness of eating alone in a nearly empty food court in Penang. You're here because Anthony Bourdain ate here once. You're partly in Penang because you liked that episode where he came here. You plan on eating at many of the George Town restaurants Bourdain ate at. Because he was cool and you are not. Well, guess what you uncool idiot. He also probably ate burgers out of a gutter while two days into a methadone program. Is that something you want as well?

The beer arrives like minor salvation. Finally, something to do: getting drunk in the syrupy, malaise-mongering heat while the overhead fans nuzzle your back. You're not even nursing your beer, you're shotting it like a speakeasy regular with burnt-out eyes. You see a thin blonde with a strawberry smoothie half her size. Probably an Instagram influencer. You watch a stocky white man order a bucket of Tiger beers all for himself. Also probably an influencer. You're not even a normal person who follows the pied pipering of social media — you're following syndicated broadcast recommendations like a prehistoric degenerate.

You can't decide what to order. Too many stalls. Paralysis of choice. You'd have to leave the table for that. You'd have to have a modicum of mental strength. Oh, look. A baby cockroach just scuttled across the tabletop and sipped on some of the beer you spilt. A curious sesame seed with antennae. Wonder if it'll get as drunk as me tonight? And feel as goddamn awkward.

Shit. Now a large Malaysian Chinese family with children have settled into the tables around you. Why did they have to flank you when there are so many other empty tables? And now you find the place too busy. And bad Christmas jingles are playing over the speakers. Too many patrons! Too much humanity! When will this constant tension, the inexorable forces of aversion and craving, the cycles of samsara end?

Time to make a move. Get up and order some chicken skewers. It's better than nothing.

## 8 December 2019 — Kuala Lumpur

Kuala Lumpur!

Didn't have the strongest recollections of the city (I remember being impressed by the Islamic Arts Museum and the Batu Caves were a highlight, obviously) so was keen to sniff it out some more.

Tried to follow urban planner Alain Bertaud's dictum of learning a city by just randomly walking around it for a few hours. A terrible idea in this climate. At one point, while I was trudging up a hill to a Buddhist temple near Little India in the midday swelter, I wondered whether heatstroke was a distinct possibility.

I am thinking more favourably of KL on my second visit. Next time I have a layover here, I'll extend my stay a full 24 hours. For one thing, I was surprised by how many streets and landmarks I could still remember. I have strong anxieties about my ability to form stable memories (something perhaps best discussed another time) so realising that I never 'forgot' the city was a gentle surprise.

A verdant, 24-hour city with cheap eats, an even cheaper foreign workforce, and a patchy light rail system that shrieks like a banshee. It ranks strongly on global liveability and safety indexes relative to other parts of Southeast Asia and you can tell. City of the muddy confluence: Singapore's brusquer, grubbier step-brother (look, I don't have time to edit all these thoughts). Significantly more construction everywhere than I remembered. Plus, as far as I could tell, the traffic was not this terrifyingly awful thing people kept telling me about.

The old colonial and Malay Islamic architecture scattered throughout the city is eye-catching. There's also something cosy and innocently pleasant about travelling around a rich, cosmopolitan city which caters to a significant Muslim population. I'll leave it as an exercise to the reader to determine the potentially problematic implications in that statement.

Condos and affluent shopping abound — three of the world's ten largest shopping malls, in fact. Last time I was in KL, a bloke I met told me how the department stores in Malaysia were like Star Trek holodecks, with every pixel perfectly placed. It was an influential off-hand remark for me. Even now, whenever I walk into any department store in the world, I often think to myself 'welcome to the fuckin' Holodeck.'

As I was walking along the miserable-looking Klang River — more like a glorified toxic storm drain — I saw a large fish splash about. I couldn't believe it! In that kind of muddy pollution! The endurance of life against all odds.

Night two: Scored a free ticket to see a martial arts tournament in Kuala Lumpur! Got to watch grown men (and women) beat the living bejeezus out of each other for our entertainment.

It was everything I should've expected but didn't. The hype master. Ring girls with t-shirt cannons. Fighters with nicknames like "Babyface Killer" and "Shadowcat". The stern Japanese referee who clearly wanted 'a good clean fight'. Lots of bleeding noses, ears, and eyebrows. And the crowd obviously going wild whenever the Malaysian fighters went up.

I definitely preferred the kickboxing fights to the mixed martial arts ones. Too much hugging in the latter.

Onwards to Penang now.

## 5 December 2019 — Project Peregrinus

Dear friends.

As many of you know, I am about to embark on a seven-months-plus trip around the world.

"Project Peregrinus" (as I've dubbed this personal life project) has been in the works for much of 2019, with all the endless research and anxiety and excitement and self-doubt that has accompanied it. I'm a mildly seasoned traveller but I've never attempted something quite like this. My current plan is two months in India via Malaysia, followed by Egypt, Israel, and Jordan, and then onwards to continental Europe and beyond. I've planned and planned and planned, knowing fully well that none of it will go as expected.

Travel is one of my great passions in life — quite possibly my greatest passion. I have so many things I want to do on this trip. I want to see great works of art and explore major world cities. I want to visit some of the holiest sites on earth and some of the most ancient. I want to mainly travel by train and bus. I want to scuba dive and I want to meditate. I want to hike and I want to photograph. I want to read and I want to write. And by god, I want to seize the world's reins while I still can.

Of course, I have no illusions about what it'll be like. For one thing, I tend to travel on the less comfortable side, and for another, I am travelling solo. I know for a fact that I will experience many lows to complement the highs. That is simply how these things go. There will be days where I will feel intense loneliness. There will be days where I will fall sick. I will have moments of weakness and moments of failure. And I'll meet some terrible people just as certainly I'll meet incredible people. That is simply how everything goes.

Travel doesn't automatically make you a better or smarter or more interesting person. It certainly can't substitute for a personality. Travel doesn't guarantee anything, quite frankly. But there is "something" there, I think. Some kind of essence, some type of crude matter that I want to hold onto and work with.

I've been told by people (perhaps only half-jokingly) on many occasions that this trip is my "quarter-life crisis" and a chance for me to "find my true self". Bloody hell! Clichés abound! But I will be level with you: I also do need a break. Over the last little while, my mental health hasn't been the best. I'm a bit lost with my career at the moment and recently I've been dealing with heartache. And I've felt burdened by personal disappointments, some lasting years. Travel can't "fix" any of these, but a respite is always nice. Yes, a respite might do some good.

As you can no doubt tell, I am firmly putting on my writer's hat. At the very least, I intend to thoroughly document my trip and post way too many photos and errant thoughts on Facebook and elsewhere. I wish to apologise ahead of time for the quantity and/or quality of what is about to come...

Anyway, I think that's all I have to say for now. Time for one more night of fretful sleep in Melbourne and then it's onwards and, hopefully, upwards.

## 27 January 2019 — Shanghai

Shanghai, what a metropolis. The largest city in China and the second most populous city proper on the planet. Arriving into the city from the smoggy industrial sprawls of the Jiangsu Province was a godsend. Crisp, clean air! Here, the air is dense with something other than the belchings of coal-fired plants and light manufactories. It's redolent of international finance, fashion, retail, professional services and real estate - the parfum of global capital flows!

When Anthony Bourdain called Shanghai the most materialistic place on earth, he wasn't kidding. During my short stay, I had the opportunity to visit a private yacht club and peek into its decadent wine cellars. I strolled into a Louis Vuitton showcase at the Exhibition Centre just for kicks. I drank Hennessy XO Cognac (mixed with sweet tea!) at the VIP table of a booming nightclub with my father and his 40+ year old investor colleagues (don't ask). This city is a relentless, gilded cynosure.

Some other, disjointed thoughts:

- Unlike many other urban centres in the Yangtze River Delta region, where economic growth has supplanted aesthetics with hedgerows of copy-pasted Soviet style buildings, Shanghai is very pleasing to the eye. Is it as visually attractive as other global cities (e.g. New York, Paris, Hong Kong)? Probably not. But after spending so much time in banal, industrially stamped Chinese cities to the north, seeing the old European banks, consulates, clubs and trading houses of the Shanghai Bund was deeply refreshing. Likewise, the French Concession is a lovely area to walk around. Hate to admit this, but a colonial history can, at the very least, make a city nicer to look at.
- Even so, the Chinese way of unleashing cycles of creative (and un-creative) destruction has left its mark. Consider the stunning, glittering Jing'an Temple on West Nanjing Road. On paper, sure, this Buddhist temple has existed since the 12th century. In reality, it was repeatedly destroyed and rebuilt over the centuries and was, at its nadir, converted into a plastics factory during the Cultural Revolution. The current temple, as grand as it is, is only 35 years old. Such is the case with many "historical" sites in Mainland China.
- I found Shanghai fairly walkable, which I like in my cities. Also, it's hard to become even remotely lost with so much English everywhere.
- The people are big city brusque, as you'd expect. But they're not pathologically rude, like the denizens of Beijing. Shanghaiers also don't seem to spit on the streets that much (an infamous habit of many PRC pedestrian, I'm afraid to say). The subways (which are, as you'd expect, seamless networks of efficiency) have all the usual public transport PSAs: where to stand, how to let passengers off first, how to be a courteous commuter. Yet no one follows them. They do in Taipei. They do in Tokyo. They do in Singapore. They do in Hong Kong. But China is still China and does things China's way.
- If you want to do your counterfeit shopping, I suggest you hop on the train to a nearby city. In Shanghai, given it's a global hub of big brand retail, you might accidentally buy the real thing. Still, you find the occasional oddities. I came across a few stores on Old Shanghai Street selling 'authentic mammoth tusks'. Right.
- The standards for Shanghai police officers seem surprisingly low — especially given how the city's the crowning jewel of the government. Some of them were real Paul Blart mall cop-ish guys, standing around doing very little.
- Shanghai Museum was wonderful. Free too. Strolling around the building, I got a real sense of just how differently societies can appreciate art and culture. Consider, for example, the Chinese calligraphy wing of the museum. While largely devoid of western tourists, there were throngs of Chinese visitors crowding around particular scrolls and taking photos of ancient Chinese poems. Could you transpose such admiration onto the western world? An analogy would be if the English romantic poets were also famed for their breathtaking penmanship and people travelled from far and wide to a museum in London just to gaze upon their original manuscripts.
- I matched with more white women on Tinder in Shanghai over a two-day period than the entirety of my time using it in Melb. Go figure.
- It's a place where high and low-tech intermingle incessantly. Cash is clearly on the way out, since it seems you can pay at most places in Shanghai with only your smartphone via Wechat Pay or Alipay. I was often the only one paying in cash in some places. Yet everywhere you go, there are also simple street vendors, dealing in grubby notes.
- China's Peppa Pig obsession is real. I don't understand it. That pink porky fuck is everywhere.
- A quick note on food: The conversations about food in China would put most western foodies to shame. At the dinner table, I'd listen to people who were by no means social elites or intense connoisseurs talk endlessly about the best way to cook this breed of chicken, the best age to slaughter a piglet, the best region in China to source that vegetable, etc., etc. You could dedicate an entire lifetime to exploring the various cuisines of China and not even come close to covering it all.
- Did it matter that my Standard Mandarin is spotty? Not really. With so much intra-national migration in China, I heard plenty of funky accents and dialects. Also heard a lot of code switching between regional tongues and the "Beijing Standard". I could have (with a bit of effort) passed for a Chinese national, albeit a slightly strange one from a faraway province. Maybe.

## 22 January 2018 — Hong Kong

Hong Kong's a hectic town. Visit if you like fun and want to perform penitence for the Opium Wars. Some neat little things to do, aside from the loud, larger-than-life, tourist attractions:

1. Take the slow, dorky, double-decker trams across Hong Kong Island. They are lumbering turtles compared to the rabbit-paced streets around them.
2. Go to a proper local tea cafe. Drink thick, over-brewed condensed milk tea. Have a middle-aged lady shout at you in Cantonese. Point at random (but delicious) things on their huge menu and have it served to you in five minutes flat.
3. Buy dope-ass Chinese tea on the cheap. Shit's the bomb, yo. Marvel at how pu'er tea can be aged for decades and cost ridiculous amounts.
4. After going up Victoria Peak on the funicular tram, take a local double-decker bus down the mountain. Vividly imagine how you will plummet to your death as the bus driver swerves his way down the slope.

## 18 January 2018 — Singapore

Going to keep up my New Year's goal of recording down more of my thoughts, so here's a quick take on Singapore:

Second time visiting since my first trip in 2015 and my thoughts on the place have remained *largely* unchanged. Singlish is gibberish. Ticket prices for museums and galleries should be much cheaper. Public infrastructure is preternaturally efficient. The price of alcohol is ghastly - but good food is surprisingly affordable if you know where to go.

Those that know me well have likely heard my rambling critiques against putting Singapore on a pedestal as the gleaming city-upon-a-hill. Some comments I've brought up in the past:

- "Caning is a punishment over there lol." [It's a surprisingly formal process.]
- "It's effectively a one-party technophilic democracy!" [Can't dispute that.]
- "Sodomy laws are still on the books. Weird." [If largely unenforced.]
- "Is Singapore *really* that multicultural?" [In the Western liberal fusion-of-peoples-cultures-and-ideas sense of the word.]

On reflection, I think I'm just reacting to the clichéd praise that Singapore frequently (and in many cases, correctly) receives - how clean, efficient, and unbelievably safe it is, etc, etc. I like a bit of edginess and earthiness to the places I visit. Plus, I've never been a big fan of relentless technocracy and societal perfectibility (probably means I'm not a great economist). I enjoy being a contrarian, alas.

[An aside: If you want the absolute gold standard of anti-Singaporeanism, I highly recommend William Gibson's infamous 1993 *Wired* piece "Disneyland with the Death Penalty". It was so scathing, *Wired* magazine got banned for a while over there.]

Anyway, I'll simply say this: visit Singapore if you get the chance, especially since everyone's flights to Asia are going through Kuala Lumpur more and more these days. Make up your own goddamn minds.

## 16 January 2018 — South Korea

A review of South Korea based on two weeks of travel:

Seoul feels underrated as an asian metropolis compared to nearby cities like Tokyo and Shanghai. A vibrant urban powerhouse that is definitely worth a visit if you have a few days spare on your East Asia trip. Bullet points:

- Public transport and overall tourism infrastructure is world-class. This seemed to be the case outside of Seoul too, even in places that weren't as popular with western tourists. Anywhere in Korea that can't be easily reached by train can likely be reached by an impressive intercity bus network. It's worth noting that Google Maps is awful in Korea (non-Korean map apps have government data restrictions).
- The food alone is worth the price of admission (if you stay away from the super touristy areas). Bulgogi, bibimbap, Korean BBQ, and fried chicken - you know the drill. If you are unfamiliar with Korean cuisine/flavours though: do some quick research first and be prepared to eat adventurously. Street food seemed fairly hygienic. Basic Korean booze is also dirt cheap (if a tad mediocre).
- Even in sub-zero temperatures, the locals were all frightfully fashion savvy. I felt rather underdressed throughout my trip. And pack appropriately if you visit in the dead of winter, or you'll be dumb like me and catch a cold. The 1950s wool boom in NZ and Australia happened for a good reason, after all.
- The War Memorial of Korea in Seoul is impressive. And as it goes without saying, visiting the DMZ is a must (and easily arranged). That said, if you wanted to learn about South Korea during its periods of autocracy in the post-war period, you'll be hard-pressed to — wait, 'dictatorships'? What 'dictatorships'??
- Related to that point: If you are like me and love to visit grand palaces and ancient temples, prepare to be... merely appreciative. Unfortunately, many of Korea's major historical sites, including major palaces & temples in Seoul, are pallid restorations of structures destroyed by Japanese forces (in both the 16th century and in the early 1900s).
- The Korean bathhouse, or jjimjilbang, is a must-try. Once you get over the slight culture shock of casual nudity, that is. Even if you are familiar with the Japanese onsen, this is a very different experience. Some bathhouses are grubbier and less inviting than others, mind you.
- Unless you are keen on a deeper dive into Korea, I can't exactly recommend many areas outside of Seoul as 'must visits', particularly if you are pressed for time. Busan is Seoul's dirtier, more decentralised port city cousin that has a few points of interest. Jeonju might be worth a day trip for its food and hanok village. Similarly, Gyeongju is worth a day for its ruins - but only if you are weird like me and interested in a somewhat obscure (in world history terms) ancient Asian dynasty.
- Final point: Internet memes aside, I have no reference point for K-pop whatsoever. Korean cinema, on the other hand, is absolutely on fire at the moment. Bong Joon-ho, Park Chan-wook, Lee Chang-dong. Go watch a Korean movie right now.

## 26 January 2014 — Some reflections on my travels

*Written while jetlagged in Sydney.*

### Proem

Layover limbo at Abu Dhabi. This transit point in the nowhere, this artifice on the dunes, *ex nihilo* in the desert. Slivered on this foreign time zone, I find that the meaning of sunrise and sunset have become blurred like the contrails of aeroplanes long passed.

Look at all us interlopers! Slumpbacked on pleather seats, glaze-eyed on travelators. Round and round the whirling dervishes go. Around the pillars of Duty Free and shitty WiFi we dance and spin. Clutching handfuls of AED from the UAE in the ADI, round and round we go.

### Paris

Down and out in Paris on a winter's day. It is our lunchtime and we are sitting on a park bench under the phallic majesty of the Eiffel Tower, eating baguettes, fromage, and pâté like the worthless vagabonds we are. We clutch cold hunks of cheap sustenance between frozen fingers and shovel them between our chapped lips. Mmmm... It's so... f-f-fucking... d-de-delicious...

The Parisians that stroll past look as well dressed as always.

### Berlin

Standing behind the yellow line at Alexanderplatz, I wait for the next scheduled arrival of German efficiency. A drunk man in a khaki jacket staggers around on the platform, holding a plastic bag full of empty beer bottles.

A couple of the bottles roll onto the tracks. The drunk man jumps down onto the tracks and starts casually picking them up. There are shouts and screams and two people rush to pull him out of there just as the next train pulls into the station. I swear the S-Bahn almost hits him. He curses at them and then walks away, laughing.

'Jesus Christ', I say. The German man standing next to me agrees. 'Crazy', he says.

### Zurich

I have boarded an early morning train for northern Italy. A gaunt Swiss man in a woollen beanie drops his coffee on the floor. 'Merde!' he cried. 'Merde!' And as if to reaffirm the nature of the world, he repeats in English: 'Shit! Shit!'. The coffee water laps against the heels of my boots. I move forward a seat.

### Turin

There once was a Christmas in Italy  
Among the ANU student nobility.  
With pasta and wine  
And a Russian man, we dined,  
Only time will reduce alcohol toxicity.

### Florence

It is my final night in Florence. I am sitting on the slopes of the Palazzo Pitti, smoking and drinking beer. Only a handful of stars pierce the Tuscan cloudscape. The cobblestone streets feel old and sleepy. She offers me another cigarette. This is the happiest I have been in a long time.

Florence is surely the beautiful place on Earth. I will say no more.

### Rome

Greasy pizza breakfast and Senegalese panini.

Open-air concert at the Spanish Steps. Overpriced gelato at Piazza Navona.

I wander the slick and sleepy New Year streets of Roma.

I toss two coins into Trevi Fountain.

It is sometimes good to be alone, my friend.

### Vatican City

I am the first one up to the top of St. Peter's Basilica this morning. Church bells thunder as I watch sunrise over St. Peter's Square and the city of Rome. It is a beautiful sight.

Checkmate, atheists!

### Venice

Nightfall in Venice, one must depart the narrow street squeeze of San Marco and San Polo and escape sideways into the depths of the city. Weaving through the empty canal corridors of Cannaregio and Castello, one becomes lost among the unlit walkways and dead-end bridges, lost with the lonely sound of boat creak and water lap-lapping on stone step.

Up ahead is a little square surrounded by shuttered façades of old shops. The locals here have not yet taken down the Christmas decorations. The street lamps cast a mulled, swollen haze of orange over this little square. Two children are playing soccer against a brick wall.

Have I finally found the real Venice? Or is this just another trick of the light?

### Vienna

By god! European art will be the death of me! I have too many gold-gilded medieval altarpieces, too many paintings of brutalised saints and crucified messiahs, too many hideous Christ Childs and 16th century Flemish paintings with their creepy reptile-eyed Jesuses. I close my eyes and all I see are walls lined with the Annunciations of the Lord, Madonnas with Childs, Depositions of Christ, heads of John the Baptist and porcupined Saint Sebastians. The Lance of Longinus, the Holy Sponge, Saint Veronica with her flappity flap of flapping holy cloth.

I pray for a reprieve. Anything to snap me out of this wretched continental canonicity! If only the museum cafe weren't so bloody overpriced, I might even stay here another hour...

### Budapest

A warm winter is the worst kind of winter. No snow to let the earth sleep. An ugly, sluggish world laid bare without a layer of foundation, without its seasonal make-up. Surely the mighty Magyars built their empire on the dead, snow-bitten earth, not this tepid kingdom of a fool's winter?

### Prague

Midnight on the Charles Bridge. The hawkers, pickpockets, and beggarmen of Prague have all retired for the night. Only lovers and street cleaners remain. All of us caught under the dead gaze of a menagerie of saints.

A soft presence of snowfall is caught in the columns of street light. I recall that the Turkish gentleman at the pizzeria on Karlova street told me it snowed last winter for only thirty minutes. 'It was boring', he said.

I stand around on the bridge for a few minutes, readying myself for something to happen. But nothing does.

### Dresden

I think I shall visit Dresden again in ten, twenty years. Perhaps by then the yawning gaps between the Altstadt skyline will have been finally filled up. Perhaps with time the Frauenkirche will no longer resemble the inside of a Hungarian bathhouse. Maybe even one day Neustadt will escape the clutches of its bourgeois bohème ways.

Ah yes! Elbflorenz reborn! How I would love to waltz down her wide streets once more. I would trace my fingers along the street art and ride the rumbling tram lines. I would kick through the dead leaves of Alaunpark, wander through its riverside flea market, before sailing down the Elbe herself to find out where all the pretty German girls are.
